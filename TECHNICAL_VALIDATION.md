# Technical Validation — Does the Coordination We Built Make Sense?

Answers the question directly: is the optimization trustworthy, are the
settlement numbers economically sensible, do the constraints ("the rules")
reflect real machine physics, and can we say anything about optimality
guarantees? Everything below is computed from the 2,915-hour water-value
run (`results/monthly_hourly_waterval.csv`) plus targeted cross-checks run
specifically to answer these questions. Figures referenced are in
`results/figures/waterval_*.png`, generated by `analyze_waterval_results.py`.

## 1. Optimality — are we stuck in a bad local optimum?

Two independent checks, both strengthened this session from a single
base-case spot-check to a real sample:

**Multi-start (same algorithm, different starting points).** 12 real hours,
chosen to span all 4 months and the low/median/high demand deciles within
each month. Each solved from 5 randomly perturbed starting points.
**Result: 12/12 hours converged to the same objective across all 5 starts,
maximum relative spread 2.5×10⁻¹²** — indistinguishable from floating-point
noise. IPOPT is landing on the same KKT point regardless of where it starts,
across a genuinely diverse sample of real operating conditions, not just one
hand-picked hour.

**Differential evolution (different algorithm entirely — gradient-free,
population-based, no relation to IPOPT's interior-point method).** Re-run
under the corrected water-value convention on the base case:
IPOPT = 3169.9914 EUR/h, DE = 3170.0153 EUR/h — **a 0.00075% gap**, even
tighter than the earlier (pre-fix) check's 0.92%.

**What this does and doesn't prove.** Neither check is a formal global-
optimality certificate — no NLP solver gives one for a non-convex AC-OPF.
But two independent methods landing on the same answer from many different
starting conditions is real, meaningful evidence against the specific
failure mode that would undermine every other number in this study (IPOPT
silently reporting a bad local optimum as "optimal"). This is now a
defensible claim, not an assumption.

## 2. Where the losses actually come from, and whether they're avoidable

See `waterval_loss_composition.png` and `waterval_avoidable_loss.png`.

**Network losses dominate machine losses roughly 2:1.** Mean network
(line I²R) loss is 0.0459 MW; the four generators' own stator+field copper
losses sum to 0.0208 MW. Total system loss ≈0.067 MW.

**Within machine losses, field (rotor) loss dominates stator (armature)
loss heavily** — visible directly in the figure. This is expected: field
loss scales with `(V + X_s·Q/V)²`, which is far more sensitive to Q than
stator loss's `(P²+Q²)/V²` term, and every machine is dispatched with
positive Q well above its own loss-minimising Q\* (see below) to support
the network.

**Is it avoidable?** Compared each machine's actual dispatched loss against
what it would lose at the *same P and V* but at its own Q\* (the
loss-minimising point). On average, **25–51% of each machine's own loss is
"above Q\*"** — G1: 37.8%, G2: 25.8%, G3: 50.9%, G4: 25.7%. This is not
waste or a bug: it is machines being pushed away from their own private
optimum because the *system* needs that reactive power somewhere on the
network (voltage support, reduced line current, reduced reactive import at
the interface). **This gap — the extra machine loss above Q\* — is exactly
what `C_g^Q` is supposed to price**, and it is a real, quantifiable
physical cost, not a modeling artifact. This is a good, concrete answer to
"can we avoid this loss": partially, if you didn't need the generators to
support the network — but then you'd pay more in interface reactive import
and/or run higher network losses instead. It's a substitution, not free
money left on the table.

**Does coordination actually help?** Two different, partially conflicting
answers depending on the metric:
- **Average network losses**: roughly a wash (coordinated is ~1.2 kW lower
  than baseline on average, but *higher* in 46% of hours — see
  `RESULTS_ANALYSIS.md` §2). Coordination trades losses for reactive-import
  savings, not a clean loss reduction.
- **Peak congestion risk**: coordination clearly helps here.
  `waterval_line_loading.png` shows baseline has a long tail up to ~96%
  line loading in high-demand hours, while coordinated dispatch never
  exceeds ~47% across all 2,915 hours — coordination is actively using
  voltage support to keep the feeder trunk out of trouble at peak, even
  though it doesn't systematically reduce average losses. **This is
  probably the more honest "coordination helps" claim for the slide** —
  frame it as congestion management, not loss reduction.

## 3. Dispatch sense-check — does the P/Q allocation make physical sense?

See `waterval_dispatch_by_month.png`.

- **G1 (largest, best-located) is the only machine that regularly produces
  above its own P floor** — up to 1.9 MW against a 1.2 MW minimum in
  January. G2, G3, G4 sit essentially exactly at their P floor in every
  single hour of the 2,915-hour run. This is the water-value convention
  working as designed (§5 of `RESULTS_ANALYSIS.md`): active energy has no
  extra value once `c_g^P=λ_E`, so only the machine whose local generation
  measurably cuts losses/import cost ever produces more than the floor.
- **Reactive dispatch tracks the season correctly**: G1's and G3's Q both
  roughly double from summer (Jun/Jul, ~0.3–0.4 MVAr) to winter (Jan,
  ~2.1–2.6 MVAr), matching the real CINELDI demand's own winter/summer
  ratio. December sits between the two, also correctly.
- **G3 (6 MVA) delivers *more* reactive power than G1 (8 MVA) in most
  months** despite being the smaller machine — not obviously wrong (G3
  reuses G1's electrical "type," and is on the separate, less-relieved
  feeder 2), but worth a specific look if you want to explain it on a
  slide: is this location, machine type, or something else driving it.
- **G2 and G4 sit close to zero Q year-round**, occasionally slightly
  negative (absorbing) — both are the smaller "Type B" units with worse
  field-loss coefficients; the optimizer correctly avoids asking them for
  much reactive power.

## 4. Settlement economics — do capacity, variable, and hybrid payments make sense?

**Magnitude sense-check against the real Statnett rate.** The real fos §15
capacity rate (0.00248 EUR/MVArh, annualized = 21.7 EUR/MVA/year) applied to
this fleet totals only **159 EUR over the whole 2,915-hour, 4-month run** —
against a total reactive service cost of 1,642 EUR over the same period.
**That's 9.7% cost recovery from the real capacity mechanism alone.**
Nodal utilisation pricing does far better: 80.7% recovery. Hybrid
(capacity+utilisation stacked): 90.4%. See `waterval_scheme_comparison.png`.

This is not a modeling artifact — it's a direct, quantified consequence of
two real facts already documented in the code: (a) fos §15's rate is small
in absolute terms, and (b) it doesn't even apply to units this size in
practice (all sub-10MVA, distribution-connected). **This is probably the
single strongest, most defensible number in the whole study for answering
"how should reactive power be incentivized" — the current real mechanism
underpays by roughly 10x for exactly the population this project is about.**

**Assumed (Case A) vs physically-derived (Case B) price magnitude.**
Re-checked directly (not just "different shape," which was already known):
Potter's assumed price (0.1×λ_E = 7.0 EUR/MVArh, flat) is **~28x larger**
than the physically-derived price at typical load (median 0.248 EUR/MVArh
for G1). See `waterval_case_a_vs_b_price.png`. This is a materially
different finding from "the shapes differ" — the assumed convention isn't
just shaped wrong, it's roughly an order of magnitude too expensive for
*this* machine/network combination. Worth stating plainly: this doesn't
mean Potter's convention is "wrong" in general (their FERC citation is
about a different market context), it means it's a poor fit for this
specific hydro fleet's actual physics, which is exactly the point the
whole project is making.

**Does "capacity vs. variable" as a *mechanism design* make sense?**
Yes, structurally — it mirrors real ancillary-service markets (capacity
payments for standing availability, utilisation payments for delivered
service, e.g. PJM's stacked capacity+energy markets, cited in
`src/settlement.py`). What's now demonstrated, not just asserted, is that
under REAL Norwegian rates, the capacity leg alone is economically
negligible for this fleet — the utilisation leg is doing essentially all
the compensating work.

## 5. Capability constraints ("the rules") — do the limits reflect real physics?

Computed the actual capability-diagram geometry for all 4 machines
(stator circle, field circle, underexcitation ray) rather than just trusting
the equations were coded right (already verified independently by the
earlier blind review). Key finding: **the field limit genuinely is tighter
than the stator limit at each machine's own rated point** — e.g. G1 at
P=p_max: field caps Q at 3.74 pu vs. the stator circle's 4.21 pu. The rule
is not toothless in principle.

**It just never gets exercised in this study**, because no generator is
ever dispatched anywhere near its own P_max under realistic demand (§3
above — they sit at their floor almost always). Combined with the earlier
finding that installed capacity exceeds real feeder demand in most hours,
this fully explains — with numbers, not just a shrug — why the "G1 hits
its field limit" story from the original toy-system design never appears
in the real-data version. The rule is fine; the fleet is oversized relative
to what this particular demand data ever asks of it.

## 6. Bottom line

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Is IPOPT finding real optima, not stuck locally? | Yes — 12/12 multi-start hours agree to 1e-12; independent DE method agrees to 0.0008% |
| Do the capability constraints reflect real machine physics? | Yes, verified geometrically; they just aren't binding at this fleet's demand levels |
| Does coordination reduce losses? | Not reliably (46% of hours it's worse) — but it does reliably cut peak congestion |
| Is the real capacity payment mechanism adequate? | No — 9.7% cost recovery, a genuine, quantified policy finding |
| Is the assumed-cost convention (Case A) a good stand-in for this fleet? | No — ~28x too high, not just a different shape |
| Does the P/Q dispatch pattern make sense? | Yes — seasonal tracking, floor-pinning under water-value, and machine-type differences are all explainable |

Nothing above required walking back a result — every check either
confirmed the model is behaving correctly, or turned a vague worry ("does
this make sense?") into a specific, quotable, defensible number. That's the
right place to be two days out from the deadline.
